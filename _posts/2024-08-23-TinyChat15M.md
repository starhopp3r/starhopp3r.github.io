Large language models (LLMs) such as GPT-4 and Google’s Gemini mark a major advance in artificial intelligence. Models with hundreds of billions of parameters, including GPT-3 with 175 billion and Google’s PaLM 2 with 540 billion, handle tasks from creative writing to code generation. But these capabilities cost a great deal. Training GPT-3, for example, required about 3640 petaflop per second days and cost millions of dollars, while running it efficiently demanded over 700 GB of VRAM.

![img](/assets/img/metadatacen.jpeg)
_Meta's AI Research SuperCluster (Photo: Meta Platforms Inc.)_

These resource demands and their environmental cost have encouraged work on small language models (SLMs). SLMs perform specific tasks with a fraction of the resources that large models need. Training GPT-3, for example, used 1287 MWh of electricity and produced 502 metric tons of carbon emissions, equal to the annual emissions of 112 gasoline powered cars. Smaller models offer a practical, efficient option for mobile devices and edge computing platforms with limited resources.

I built TinyChat15M, a fifteen million parameter conversational language model based on the Meta Llama 2 architecture, to run on devices with as little as sixty MB of free memory. It has run successfully on a Sipeed LicheeRV Nano W, a small RISC V development board with 256 MB of DDR3 memory. Inspired by Dr. Andrej Karpathy’s llama2.c project, TinyChat15M shows how small conversational models can work effectively with few resources, making advanced AI capabilities more accessible and sustainable.

## TinyChat: A Synthetic Dataset for Short Chat Conversations

I trained TinyChat15M on TinyChat, a synthetic dataset of one million short chat conversations written in BASIC English. The name is a backronym for British American Scientific International and Commercial English, a form of English designed for clear, simple communication. I generated the dataset with GPT 4o mini, a specialized version of GPT 4o, using mainly BASIC English words and grammar. Some non BASIC English words helped the conversations remain coherent and natural. The dataset draws on Eldan et al.'s work, TinyStories: How Small Can Language Models Be and Still Speak Coherent English, which explores how small models can produce coherent text. Following that approach, TinyChat uses a limited vocabulary and selected sentence structures to keep conversations simple and coherent.

Linguist and philosopher Charles Kay Ogden designed BASIC English as an international auxiliary language and a tool for teaching English as a second language. He introduced it in his 1930 book, Basic English: A General Introduction with Rules and Grammar. It simplifies standard English by reducing its vocabulary and grammar to a core of 850 words grouped into operations, things, picturable things, general qualities, and opposite qualities. A defined set of affixes and forms extends these word roots to express more ideas.

To generate TinyChat, I divided these 850 words into nouns, verbs, and adjectives, following an approach similar to Eldan et al.'s TinyStories paper. They used about 1500 basic words to match a typical three to four year old child's vocabulary, also grouped into nouns, verbs, and adjectives. For each story, they randomly selected one word from each group and asked the model to use all three, adding variety to the dataset.

Combining BASIC English nouns, verbs, and adjectives gave me over eight million possible word combinations. To limit generation costs, I randomly sampled one million combinations and used them in prompts for GPT 4o mini. This was the prompt template:

**System Prompt**

> You are a helpful, respectful, and honest assistant. Always aim to provide the most helpful and safe answers. Your responses should not contain any harmful, unethical, racist, sexist, toxic, dangerous, or illegal content. Ensure that your answers are socially unbiased and positive. If a question does not make sense or is not factually coherent, explain why rather than providing an incorrect answer. If you do not know the answer, do not share false information. Use only BASIC English words, along with words that a typical three to four year old would understand. Follow these specific grammar rules: add es or ies to form plural nouns, ing or ed to turn verbs into adjectives, ing or er to turn verbs into nouns, ly to turn adjectives into adverbs, and er or est to compare amounts. Use un to create opposites of adjectives. Form questions by inverting the word order with do. Use normal English changes for verbs and pronouns, create compound words from two nouns or a noun and a direction, and write measures, numbers, money, days, months, years, and clock times in English words with no symbols or special characters, except for commas and periods. Do not use any punctuation marks except for commas and periods. Spell out all numbers as simple English words. Use industry specific or scientific terms as needed, such as plural, conjugation, noun, adjective, adverb, qualifier, operator, pronoun, and directive, which are necessary in language teaching but not part of BASIC English.

**User Prompt**

> Generate a factually and logically coherent small talk conversation between a User and an Assistant, where they exchange six to ten sentences in total. Each sentence spoken by the User and the Assistant should be between ten and twenty words long. The conversation should be formatted as a single paragraph without line breaks or other textual formatting. It should begin with a `{starting}`, convey a general feeling of `{feeling}`, and conclude with a `{ending}` ending. The conversation must include the noun `{noun}`, the verb `{verb}`, and the adjective `{adjective}`. Use the following text format: `[INST]` generate User's sentence here `[INST]` generate Assistant's sentence here.

Each prompt also specified a starting tone, a general feeling, and an ending. The starting tones were greetings, questions, or suggestions. The feelings included happiness, surprise, badness, fearfulness, anger, disgust, and sadness, while endings could be conclusive, reflective, or open. Together with the word combinations, these choices varied both the tone and content of the conversations. To make them compatible with Meta's Llama 2 chat template, I enclosed them in the Llama 2 instruction tags `[INST]` and `[INST]`. The dataset is open source and hosted on Hugging Face.

## Training the TinyChat15M Small Language Model

I adapted Dr. Andrej Karpathy’s llama2.c project to build and train TinyChat15M. Its main C file, run.c, already supports chat, but I needed to change both tokenization and chat behavior for a conversational model. TinyChat already encloses user dialogue in `[INST]` and `[INST]` tags, followed by the model's responses. To help the model distinguish individual dialogues, I added Beginning of Sequence and End of Sequence tags during tokenization. Their token piece values are `<s>` and `</s>`, with token IDs 1 and 2. Training this way lets the model predict and complete sentences on its own, making it a conversational small language model that works with llama2.c's main C file.

I also made two changes to llama2.c's main C file: handling BOS tags after the model generates a sentence, and filtering out instruction tags. Because the model learns to generate these tags in sequence, it sometimes produces sentences like those spoken by users in TinyChat. It cannot inherently distinguish its responses from user sentences because the instruction tags only separate questions, suggestions, and responses. These changes let me train TinyChat15M within the llama2.c framework.

I trained TinyChat15M on Google Colab, Google's hosted Jupyter Notebook service, which provides free access to resources such as GPUs and TPUs without setup. Despite the model's small size, I used NVIDIA's A100 Tensor Core GPU through Google Colab Pro. Training ran for two hundred thousand iterations and took about five hours. It produced both the original PyTorch .pt file and a llama2.c format .bin file, which run.c uses for inference. Both models are open source and available on Hugging Face.

## Running TinyChat15M on the Sipeed LicheeRV Nano W

With 256 MB of DDR3 memory, the Sipeed LicheeRV Nano W is an ideal device for testing TinyChat15M. Running the model on this small board shows that it can support real time conversation with little memory, making the setup useful where computing and memory efficiency matter. Despite its size, the LicheeRV Nano W can run Debian, an open source Linux distribution.

![img](/assets/img/lrvnano.jpg)

TinyChat15M generated an average of 9.40 tokens per second across five runs on the LicheeRV Nano. In the video below, I play the User in a fictional workplace conversation with an Assistant powered by the model. With no prior context, the Assistant responds appropriately, much as a colleague might.

While the Assistant generates a reply, CPU usage reaches about 96 percent. Memory usage reaches about 27 percent of the available 228 MB, or around 62 MB. Within these limits, TinyChat15M produces coherent text at a reasonable speed.

The full project is open source. My [GitHub](https://github.com/starhopp3r/TinyChat) has the code to generate the synthetic TinyChat database, train TinyChat15M, and run inference. If you have suggestions or improvements, feel free to open an issue or submit a pull request. I hope the project encourages work on more general purpose conversational small language models, which could make AI more affordable and accessible to everyone.
